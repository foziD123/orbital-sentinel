HOW TO USE THIS FILE
--------------------
This is a structural blueprint, not a finished text. Each section lists the
MUST-HAVE elements — the claim, the data, the ask — in the same order a real
European Citizens' Initiative or UN-addressed petition would use them.
The water ECI succeeded because it combined an emotional rights frame with
hard law citations and very specific policy asks. Do the same here.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 1 — TITLE & SUBTITLE  (the first thing anyone reads)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MUST-HAVE: A short, declarative, human-rights-flavoured title that names the
stakes, plus a one-line subtitle that adds urgency or the "public good" frame.

SUGGESTED TITLE:
  "Low Earth Orbit is a global commons — protect it before the tipping point."

SUBTITLE OPTIONS:
  A. "Space belongs to humanity. Orbital debris is humanity's problem. We have
     the science to act. We need the political will."
  B. "Our model shows Shell C (1,000 km) has already crossed the mathematical
     tipping point. The window to act is closing."

WHY THIS FRAMING WORKS:
  The water ECI used "public good, not a commodity" to shift the debate from
  economics to rights. Here, "global commons" does the same — it invokes
  Article I of the Outer Space Treaty ("province of all mankind") and makes
  orbital debris a shared responsibility, not a private externality.

SECTION 2 — PREAMBLE / OPENING STATEMENT  (~150–200 words)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MUST-HAVE: One paragraph that (a) names who you are, (b) what you observed,
(c) why it is urgent, (d) what you are calling on whom to do.

TEMPLATE STRUCTURE:
  "We, the undersigned, [identity], having observed that [observable fact
  about the current state of LEO], alarmed that [consequence if nothing
  changes], and noting that [current law/governance is insufficient], call
  upon [the European Commission / UN COPUOS / relevant authority] to
  [high-level ask]."

KEY FACTS TO INCLUDE IN THE PREAMBLE:
  - Over 34,000 pieces of tracked debris currently orbit Earth alongside
    ~9,300 active satellites.
  - An estimated 130 million fragments too small to track but large enough
    to destroy a spacecraft are already in orbit.
  - Our bifurcation model (Orbital Sentinel) shows that the 1,000 km shell
    is operating at 109% of its mathematical tipping-point launch rate —
    already past the threshold of sustainable orbital operations.
  - Past this threshold, recovery requires active debris removal on timescales
    of decades to centuries. A launch rate cut alone cannot restore stability.
  - The global space economy is valued at over $1.8 trillion. Weather
    forecasting, Earth observation, climate monitoring, and disaster response
    all depend on continued access to Low Earth Orbit.

SECTION 3 — THE SCIENTIFIC BASIS  (the explanatory annex equivalent)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This is the "recognition by international bodies" section of the water ECI,
but replaced by our own verified scientific evidence. Include this as an
annex or a clearly labelled subsection. It is the credibility anchor.

MUST-HAVE ELEMENTS:

3.1  THE KESSLER SYNDROME — WHAT IT IS
  - Named after NASA scientist Donald Kessler (1978).
  - When orbital debris density exceeds a critical threshold, collisions
    between debris generate more debris than atmospheric drag removes,
    creating a self-sustaining cascade.
  - Once triggered, no operational change can restore the stable orbit.
    Recovery requires active debris removal over decades to centuries.
  - The cascade term in the model is quadratic in debris density (γ·D²):
    once D is large enough, this term outpaces all removal mechanisms.

3.2  WHAT THE ORBITAL SENTINEL MODEL FOUND  (cite your own results)
  - Methodology: HOPFEL bifurcation analysis (European XFEL), applied to
    three independently modelled LEO altitude shells.
  - Three model variants tested (2-species, 3-species, split-decay), 2,400
    parameter combinations, 231 passing tests: result is structurally robust.
  - The Kessler tipping point is a saddle-node fold bifurcation — not
    gradual, not reversible. Below L_fold: stable. Above L_fold: runaway.
  - Critical launch rates and current status (April 2026 data):

    | Shell  | Altitude  | L_fold (obj/yr) | L_current (obj/yr) | L/L_fold | Status |
    |--------|-----------|-----------------|---------------------|----------|--------|
    | A      | 600 km    | 25,100          | 1,329               | 0.053    | GREEN  |
    | B      | 800 km    | 670             | 206                 | 0.308    | GREEN  |
    | C      | 1,000 km  | 31.5            | 34.4                | 1.091    | RED    |

    Shell C is already past its fold. The 1,000 km band — home to Iridium,
    OneWeb, and legacy constellations — is operating in the runaway regime.

3.3  WHY THIS IS IRREVERSIBLE (the asymmetry argument — central to the petition)
  - A Hopf bifurcation can be backed away from: reduce the control parameter,
    recover the stable state. A saddle-node fold cannot.
  - Past L_fold, the stable equilibrium no longer exists. Reducing launch
    rate below L_fold after the fact does not restore it.
  - Recovery is only possible through active debris removal, on timescales
    governed by the slow atmospheric drag clock of the shell:
      * 800 km (Shell B): decades to clear
      * 1,000 km (Shell C): centuries to clear
  - This asymmetry — easy to cross, impossible to uncross through policy
    alone — is the mathematical reason the tipping point demands preventive
    action, not reactive cleanup.

3.4  EARLY WARNING INDICATORS (showing we can still act)
  - Three early-warning signals precede the fold:
      1. Recovery time after perturbation grows without bound (critical slowing down).
      2. Variance of the debris population in a sliding window inflates.
      3. Lag-1 autocorrelation of D(t) approaches 1.
  - These signals are observable with current Space-Track data.
  - Shell B is currently GREEN (L/L_fold = 0.308) with wide margin.
  - Shell A is GREEN (L/L_fold = 0.053) — wide margin, strong drag.
  - Shell C is RED — the early-warning window has passed for this shell.
  - This means: Shell A and B can still be protected. Every year of inaction
    narrows that window.

3.5  WHAT PUTS SATELLITES AT RISK (connect to everyday services)
  - Earth observation satellites (Copernicus, Landsat, NOAA): 500–800 km,
    within Shell A and B ranges.
  - Weather/climate monitoring (EUMETSAT): depends on LEO access.
  - Broadband connectivity constellations: Starlink (550 km), OneWeb (1,200 km).
  - Disaster response, maritime monitoring, agricultural sensing.
  - A cascade in Shell B (800 km) — the historically most congested shell,
    site of the 2009 Iridium-Cosmos collision — would directly threaten all
    of the above.

SECTION 4 — THE POLICY GAP  (why existing frameworks are insufficient)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MUST-HAVE: Show that you know the existing rules, that they are real but
insufficient, and that they are voluntary where they need to be binding.
The water ECI did this by noting States had obligations they were failing to
meet. Here the equivalent is: obligations exist in treaty law but enforcement
is absent.

4.1  EXISTING INTERNATIONAL LAW — THE GAP BETWEEN PRINCIPLE AND ENFORCEMENT
  - Outer Space Treaty (1967), Article I: outer space is "the province of all
    mankind." No state may claim sovereignty. The orbital environment is a
    global commons by international law.
  - Outer Space Treaty, Article VI: states bear "international responsibility"
    for national space activities, including by non-governmental entities.
  - ITU Radio Regulations: govern frequency and orbital slot allocation but
    do not address debris or carrying capacity.
  - UN COPUOS Long-term Sustainability Guidelines (2019): 21 voluntary
    guidelines. No enforcement mechanism. No compliance reporting requirement.
  - IADC Debris Mitigation Guidelines: technical best practice from space
    agencies. Non-binding. Widely ignored by commercial operators.

4.2  EXISTING INITIATIVES — PROMISING BUT INSUFFICIENT IN SCALE
  - ESA Zero Debris Charter: voluntary commitment by 130+ organisations to
    design debris-free spacecraft by 2030. Not legally binding. No verification.
  - FCC 5-Year Deorbit Rule (2022): applies to US-licensed satellites only.
    No equivalent rule in EU, China, Russia, or India — the other major
    launch nations.
  - ClearSpace-1 (ESA): active removal of one Vega-C adapter. One object.
    ESA estimates ~3,000 objects pose significant risk.
  - Astroscale ELSA-M: commercial debris removal for Starlink-class objects.
    Promising technology, no mandatory deployment requirement.
  - CONCLUSION: We have voluntary charters, single national regulations, and
    demonstration missions. We do not have binding multilateral rules keyed
    to the carrying capacity of each shell. The science exists to set those
    limits. The political will does not yet.

SECTION 5 — THE ASKS  (specific, numbered, actionable)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MUST-HAVE: The water ECI had three clear asks to the European Commission.
Ours should have 4–6 numbered asks. Each ask should be concrete enough that
a policymaker knows exactly what to legislate, and ambitious enough that
it closes the gap identified in Section 4.

CALL ON THE EUROPEAN COMMISSION / UN COPUOS TO:

1. ESTABLISH BINDING SHELL-SPECIFIC LAUNCH CAPS
   Commission the European Space Agency and an independent scientific panel
   to compute maximum sustainable launch rates (L_fold equivalents) for each
   LEO altitude band. Enact binding EU regulations — and advocate for
   equivalent UN COPUOS resolutions — that cap new launch licenses when a
   shell's current launch rate exceeds 80% of its carrying capacity
   (the amber threshold). Shells operating above 95% of carrying capacity
   (red threshold) should face a moratorium on new deployments until the
   debris population is reduced through active removal.

2. MANDATE UNIVERSAL DEORBIT WITHIN 5 YEARS FOR ALL NEW LEO SATELLITES
   Extend the FCC's 5-year post-mission deorbit rule to all satellites
   licensed by EU member states, and negotiate a binding multilateral
   agreement under COPUOS that applies the same standard globally.
   Voluntary charters do not apply to the operators that pose the greatest
   risk. Binding deorbit timelines do.

3. INTRODUCE EXTENDED PRODUCER RESPONSIBILITY FOR ORBITAL DEBRIS
   Operators who generate debris — through collisions, explosions, or
   non-compliant disposal — should bear financial liability for its
   removal, proportional to the debris count and altitude-shell decay time.
   At 1,000 km, a single fragmentation event produces debris that persists
   for centuries. The operator who caused it should fund the cleanup.
   Create an EU Orbital Cleanup Fund financed by launch licensing fees,
   ring-fenced for active debris removal missions.

4. CREATE AN INDEPENDENT ORBITAL SUSTAINABILITY WATCHDOG
   Establish a permanent EU body — or support a UN COPUOS subsidiary — with
   the mandate and resources to (a) publish annual per-shell carrying-capacity
   assessments based on current tracking data, (b) issue public early-warning
   alerts when any shell enters the amber or red zone, and (c) audit operator
   compliance with deorbit and debris-mitigation requirements. Transparency
   is the minimum floor; enforcement authority is the goal.

5. FUND ACTIVE DEBRIS REMOVAL AS A PUBLIC INFRASTRUCTURE PRIORITY
   Recognise active debris removal as critical infrastructure for the global
   digital economy, in the same category as maintaining maritime shipping
   lanes or air traffic management corridors. Allocate dedicated public
   funding — through the EU Space Programme or Horizon Europe — for at least
   10 large-object removal missions per year from shells operating above the
   amber threshold. The commercial sector cannot scale debris removal fast
   enough without this initial public investment to de-risk the market.

6. OPEN REAL-TIME ORBITAL DENSITY DATA TO THE PUBLIC
   Mandate that Space-Track.org and equivalent national registries provide
   fully public, machine-readable, real-time data on tracked object counts,
   altitudes, and decay predictions, for every LEO shell. The scientific
   community, the press, and citizens cannot hold operators or regulators
   accountable for what they cannot see. Orbital density should be public
   data — like air quality or sea surface temperature.

SECTION 6 — URGENCY STATEMENT  (the closing argument)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MUST-HAVE: A short, emotionally resonant closing that frames the irreversibility
of the tipping point and the asymmetry of action vs. inaction. The water ECI
closed with the human dignity argument. Ours closes with the irreversibility
argument — the scientific result that makes this different from ordinary
pollution problems.

SUGGESTED CLOSING PARAGRAPH:
  "Most environmental problems can be undone if we act in time.
  Orbital debris is different. Our model shows that the Kessler tipping point
  is a mathematical fold — a one-way door. Crossing it does not produce a
  worse but reversible orbit. It produces an unusable one.

  Shell C at 1,000 km has already crossed that line. The debris there will
  persist for centuries regardless of what we do today. We cannot save Shell C
  in the short term. We can still save Shells A and B.

  Every year of inaction is a year of narrowing margin. Every constellation
  added to a shell approaching its limit is a step toward a door that closes
  behind it. We have the science to know where the door is. We have the
  technology to slow our approach. We need the law to stop us walking through."

SECTION 8 — TONE & FRAMING NOTES  (for the team writing the final draft)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- DO frame LEO as a global commons. The Outer Space Treaty already does this.
  We are not inventing a new legal concept — we are enforcing an existing one.

- DO anchor every claim in either our model output or a named, citable source
  (ESA SER 2024, FCC docket, Space-Track GP catalog, Aerospace Corp report).
  The water ECI cited UN resolutions extensively. We cite our own peer-reviewed
  methodology (HOPFEL) and public agency data.

- DO use the irreversibility argument early and often. This is what
  distinguishes orbital debris from most pollution problems and justifies
  the urgency and specificity of the asks.

- DO NOT overstate the certainty of the 1,000 km RED finding. Our model
  uses literature-calibrated parameters and 2022–2024 launch rate averages.
  Say: "our model indicates Shell C is operating at or past its tipping-point
  launch rate" — not "Shell C has definitely crossed the Kessler threshold."

- DO NOT ask for things that have no legal mechanism. Each ask in Section 5
  is achievable through existing EU regulatory channels (launch licensing,
  spectrum regulation, Horizon Europe funding) or COPUOS resolutions.

- TONE: authoritative but not alarmist. The water ECI succeeded in part
  because it was sober, cited, and specific. Panic does not move bureaucrats.
  Evidence + clear asks + legal hook does.
